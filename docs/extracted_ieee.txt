 Abstract— This  paper  presents  CheynTech,  a  proposed  web-based  Online  Ordering  and  Delivery  Management  System  intended  for  Cheyn’s  Gadgets,  a  local  gadget  retailer  that  currently  operates  solely  through  walk-in  transactions  and  informal,  manual  inventory  tracking.  The  system  aims  to  enable  customers  to  browse  a  digital  product  catalog,  place  orders  online  for  either  in-store  pickup  or  staff-coordinated  local  delivery,  and  monitor  order  status  in  real  time.  Development  will  follow  an  Agile  (Scrum)  methodology,  employing  Figma  for  wireframing  and  high-fidelity  prototyping,  and  a  full-stack  web  technology  comprising  HTML5,  CSS3,  Bootstrap  5,  Vanilla  JavaScript,  PHP  8.x  with  PDO,  and  MySQL  for  data  persistence.  The  platform  will  be  deployed  on  a  cPanel-based  shared  hosting  environment  with  a  registered  public  domain.  Core  planned  features  include  a  category-filtered  product  catalog  with  search,  a  shopping  cart  and  manual-payment  checkout,  an  admin  dashboard  for  product  CRUD  and  order  management,  real-time  inventory  tracking  with  automatic  stock  decrement  and  low-stock  alerts,  and  order  fulfillment  supporting  both  pickup  and  delivery  workflows.  Security  measures  will  encompass  HTTPS,  bcrypt  password  hashing,  PDO  prepared  stateaments,  and  CSRF/XSS  protection.  The  proposed  system  seeks  to  close  the  identified  digital  gap  in  Cheyn’s  Gadgets’  business  model  and  establish  a  replicable  model  for  local  retailers  transitioning  to  online  commerce.  
 Keywords— online  ordering  system,  delivery  management  system,  inventory  tracking,  PHP,  MySQL,  e-commerce,  web  application,  Agile  development  
I.  INTRODUCTION  A.  Background  Cheyn’s  Gadgets  is  a  local  gadget  retailer  that  currently  depends  on  walk-in  customer  traffic  and  manual,  informal  inventory  tracking  to  manage  its  daily  operations.   In   today’s   digital   landscape,   where  e-commerce  has  become  the  dominant  channel  for  consumer  purchasing,  businesses  without  an  online  presence  face  a  measurable  competitive  disadvantage.  National  and  global  online  platforms  have  conditioned  consumers  to  expect  the  ability  to  browse  product  catalogs,  complete  transactions,  and  arrange  delivery  from  any  internet-connected  device.  Despite  this  ongoing  shift  in  consumer  behavior,  Cheyn’s  Gadgets  has  yet  to  establish  any  functional  digital  storefront,  leaving  its  customer  reach  limited  to  walk-in  traffic  and  its  operational  records  vulnerable  to  inaccuracy  and  loss.  This  project  addresses  that  gap  by  designing,  developing,  and  deploying  a  purpose-built  Online  Ordering  and  Delivery  Management  System  that  will  modernize  the  store’s  operations  and  extend  its  market  reach.  B.  Problem  Statement  ●  No  online  product  visibility:  the  absence  of  a  digital  catalog  prevents  customers  from  browsing  available  gadgets  remotely,  placing  the  store  at  a  direct  competitive  disadvantage  against  rivals  that  maintain  online  storefronts.  ●  No  remote  purchasing:  customers  who  discover  the  store  have  no  mechanism  to  complete  a  transaction   
--- PAGE 2 ---
without  a  physical  store  visit,  restricting  all  sales  exclusively  to  walk-in  transactions.  ●  No  delivery  option:  customers  who  are  unable  to  visit  in  person  have  no  means  of  receiving  purchased  products,  limiting  the  store’s  market  reach  to  walk-in  traffic  only.  C.  Objectives  •  To  design  and  develop  a  responsive,  customer-facing  online  storefront  for  Cheyn’s  Gadgets.  D.  Significance  The  development  of  CheynTech  will  directly  address  a  verifiable  operational  gap  in  Cheyn’s  Gadgets’  current  business  model.  By  providing  a  centralized  digital  platform  for  product  browsing,  ordering,  and  delivery  coordination,  the  system  will  enable  the  store  to  expand  its  market  reach  beyond  walk-in  customers  to  online  consumers  within  the  local  service  area.  The  shift  from  informal,  manual  record-keeping  to  a  structured,  database-driven  order  and  inventory  system  will  reduce  the  risk  of  overselling,  financial  discrepancies,  and  lost  transaction  data.  Furthermore,  the  project  aims  to  demonstrate  that  community-based  micro-retailers  can  adopt  modern  e-commerce  infrastructure  using  accessible,  open-source  technologies—offering  a  replicable  model  for  other  local  businesses  seeking  to  modernize  their  operations  within  academic  project  constraints  and  limited  budgets.  E.  Scope  of  Work  1)  Included  Features  •  A  product  catalog  with  categories,  descriptions,  images,  and  pricing.  •  Customer  registration,  login,  and  profile  management.  •  A  shopping  cart  and  checkout  flow  with  manual  payment  confirmation  (no  automated  gateway).  •  An  admin  panel  with  product  CRUD  operations,  stock  management,  and  order  status  updates.  •  Real-time  stock-level  tracking  with  automatic  decrement  on  order  confirmation  and  low-stock  visibility  alerts.  •  Order  status  tracking:  Pending  →  Processing  →  Ready  for  Pickup  /  Out  for  Delivery  →  Completed.  •  Live  deployment  on  cPanel-based  shared  hosting  with  a  registered  public  domain.  •  Responsive  design  to  be  tested  at  375  px  (mobile),  768  px  (tablet),  and  1440  px  (desktop).  •  SEO  implementation:  title  tags,  meta  descriptions,  sitemap.xml,  and  robots.txt.  •  Security  measures:  HTTPS,  bcrypt  password  hashing,  PDO  prepared  statements,  XSS  and  CSRF  protection.  
2)  Excluded  Features  •  Automated  payment  gateway  integration  (e.g.,  PayMaya,  GCash,  Stripe).  
•  Automated  courier  or  logistics  integration  (e.g.,  real-time  GPS  tracking,  third-party  apps  such  as  Lalamove  or  Grab);  delivery  is  within  scope  but  will  be  manually  dispatched  by  staff.  •  Multi-branch  inventory  support.  •  A  mobile  application  (iOS/Android).  •  A  customer  reviews  and  ratings  system  (deferred  to  Phase  2).  F.  Project  Timeline  Limitations  The  project  timeline  was  limited  to  six  months  (April  2024  –  September  2024).  This  constraint  influenced  the  scope  of  features  included  in  the  initial  release  and  necessitated  prioritization  of  core  functionalities,  such  as  the  informational  pages  and  interactive  map,  over  secondary  features  such  as  e-commerce.  
II.  RELATED  WORK  /  LITERATURE  REVIEW  This  section  reviews  six  published  studies  that  inform  the  design  and  development  of  CheynTech  across  three  thematic  clusters:  (A)  web-based  systems  for  SME  digitalization,  (B)  online  ordering  and  inventory  management  systems,  and  (C)  e-commerce  adoption  in  the  Philippine  context  and  web  application  security.  Each  study  is  examined  for  its  findings  and  its  direct  applicability  to  the  proposed  system.  1)  Office  Stationery  Inventory  Management  Application  Using  the  Laravel  Framework  Jamaludin  et  al.  present  a  web-based  inventory  management  application  for  a  stationery  trading  firm  that  faced  stock  estimation  difficulties,  inventory  shortages,  and  inefficient  manual  record-keeping  [1].  Built  with  PHP,  the  Laravel  framework,  and  MySQL,  the  system  streamlines  replenishment  and  provides  real-time  stock  monitoring.  Its  structured  MVC  architecture  confirms  that  a  PHP-MySQL  stack  is  well  suited  to  inventory-heavy  retail  applications;  while  CheynTech  uses  native  PHP  with  PDO  rather  than  Laravel,  the  underlying  principle  of  separating  business  logic  from  data  access  directly  informs  CheynTech's  back-end  design .  2)  KOOP  Online  Ordering  System  Management  at  Kolej  Kediaman  Tun  Dr  Ismail,  UTHM  Yazid  and  Yasin  describe  a  web-based  platform  developed  to  replace  in-person  purchasing  at  a  university  cooperative  shop  in  Malaysia,  where  the  existing  manual  process  caused  long  queues  and  poor  
 
--- PAGE 3 ---
stock  control  [2].  Built  using  HTML,  CSS,  PHP,  JavaScript,  and  MySQL,  the  system  introduced  modules  for  authentication,  catalog  browsing,  order  management,  payment,  tracking,  and  reporting.  This  study  is  the  most  structurally  analogous  to  CheynTech:  both  address  walk-in-only  commerce  with  the  same  technology  stack  and  the  same  core  feature  set,  validating  the  technical  feasibility  of  the  proposed  approach.  3)  Comparative  Analysis  of  SQL  Injection  Defense  Mechanisms:  PDO,  PVT,  and  ART  Choi,  Jung,  and  Ko  present  a  comparative  experimental  analysis  of  three  SQL  injection  defense  mechanisms,  evaluated  against  50  categorized  payloads  in  a  PHP-MySQL  test  environment  [3].  The  study  finds  that  PDO  prepared  statements  effectively  prevent  structural  SQL  injection  by  parameterizing  queries  so  user-supplied  input  is  never  interpreted  as  executable  SQL.  This  peer-reviewed  finding  directly  validates  CheynTech's  choice  of  PDO  prepared  statements  as  its  primary  SQL  injection  countermeasure,  though  the  study  evaluates  the  mechanism  in  isolation  rather  than  within  a  deployed  retail  system.  4)  Development  of  a  Web-Based  Information  System  to  Digitalize  Florist  SME  Operations  Flores-Alhaddad  and  Reyes  document  the  design  and  evaluation  of  an  Inventory  Management  System  for  a  Philippine  florist  SME  that  previously  relied  on  paper  logs  and  spreadsheets  [4].  Built  using  CodeIgniter  4  (MVC)  and  MySQL,  the  system  improved  inventory  accuracy  from  78%  to  97.3%,  cut  stockouts  by  45%,  and  compressed  report  generation  from  hours  to  under  ten  seconds.  This  study  directly  informs  CheynTech's  inventory  module  —  particularly  real-time  stock  decrement  and  low-stock  alerts  —  and  validates  the  feasibility  of  web-based  commerce  infrastructure  for  Philippine  micro-retailers  operating  under  resource  constraints.  5)  Determinants  of  E-Commerce  Adoption  of  Philippine  Businesses  Quimba  and  Calizo  investigate  the  factors  influencing  e-commerce  adoption  among  Philippine  businesses  using  ASPBI  data,  identifying  firm  size,  industry  type,  and  IT  infrastructure  availability  as  primary  determinants  [5].  The  study  finds  that  micro  and  small  enterprises  —  the  category  Cheyn's  Gadgets  falls  into  —  show  significantly  lower  adoption  rates  than  larger  firms,  pointing  to  a  persistent  digital  divide  among  local  retailers.  This  provides   the   macroeconomic   justification   for  
CheynTech's  development,  though  the  study  identifies  the  gap  without  proposing  a  system-level  solution.  6)  E-Commerce  Adoption  and  Its  Impact  on  the  Performance  of  Women-Led  MSMEs  in  Metro  Manila  Bacasmas,  Carlos,  and  Katigbak  examine  how  e-commerce  adoption  affects  the  performance  of  women-led  MSMEs  in  Metro  Manila,  finding  that  adoption  correlates  positively  with  sales  growth,  customer  base  expansion,  and  process  efficiency,  and  that  COVID-19  significantly  accelerated  adoption  [6].  This  complements  Quimba  and  Calizo  by  demonstrating  measurable  performance  benefits  when  local  MSMEs  successfully  adopt  e-commerce,  lending  empirical  support  to  the  outcomes  anticipated  from  CheynTech's  deployment  —  though  its  findings  are  drawn  from  Metro  Manila  and  not  yet  tested  in  a  provincial  retail  context  like  Cheyn's  Gadgets. A.  Requirement  Analysis  User  requirements  were  gathered  through  a  combination  of  methods:  online  surveys  targeting  potential  tourists  and  investors  (n  =  47  respondents),  semi-structured  interviews  with  six  local  tourism  officials  and  seafood  business  owners  in  Capiz,  and  a  comparative  analysis  of  the  competing  tourism  websites  reviewed  in  Section  II.  Survey  and  interview  data  were  thematically  coded  to  derive  a  prioritized  backlog  of  user  stories,  which  anchored  the  subsequent  design  and  development  sprints.  The  full  survey  instrument  is  reproduced  in  Appendix  A.  B.  Design  Tools  Figma  was  used  for  wireframing,  interactive  prototyping,  and  UI/UX  design.  This  collaborative,  cloud-based  design  tool  facilitated  efficient  design  iterations  and  allowed  stakeholders  to  leave  inline  feedback  on  mock-ups  without  requiring  local  software  installation,  shortening  the  feedback  loop  between  design  reviews. For  the  back  end,  Node.js  with  the  Express.js  framework  handled  RESTful  API  requests,  authentication,  and  server-side  business  logic.  Data  persistence  was  managed  using  a  MongoDB  NoSQL  database,  chosen  for  its  flexible  document  schema,  which  suited  the  varied  content  types  (business  listings,  blog  posts,  and  map  points). D.  Testing  Methods  Usability  testing  involved  30  participants  representing  the  target  audience,  recruited  through  convenience  sampling  from  university  students,  local  tourists,  and  small-business  owners.  Participants  completed  five  representative  tasks  —  locating  a  seafood  restaurant,  using  the  interactive  map,  reading  a  blog  article,  submitting  a  contact  inquiry,  and  switching  the  language  toggle  —  while  their  interactions  were  observed,  timed,  and  recorded  using  screen-capture  software.  Post-task,  participants  completed  the  System  Usability  Scale  (SUS)  questionnaire.  Their  qualitative  feedback  was  thematically  coded  to  identify  areas  for  improvement  in  navigation,  usability,  and  overall  user  experience.  Throughout  development,  rigorous  unit  and  integration  testing,  as  well  as  manual  cross-browser  regression  testing,  were  conducted  to  ensure  functionality  and  compatibility.

VIII.  ETHICAL  CONSIDERATIONS  The  website  was  designed  to  be  accessible  to  users  with  disabilities,  adhering  to  WCAG  2.1  AA  guidelines  where  feasible,  including  alt  text  for  images  and  keyboard-navigable  menus.  All  images  and  videos  used  were  appropriately  sourced,  licensed,  or  credited  to  their  original  owners.  User  data  privacy  was  respected  throughout  the  requirements-gathering  and  testing  process,  and  no  personal  information  was  collected  without  informed  consent  from  survey  and  interview  participants.  
IX.  CONCLUSION  This  project  successfully  fulfilled  its  objective  of  creating  a  visually  appealing  and  user-friendly  website  for  Capiz.  The  website's  clean  design  and  intuitive  navigation  ensure  accessibility  across  various  devices.  The  project  effectively  showcased  Capiz's  rich  seafood  industry  and  tourism  attractions  through  compelling  visuals  and  detailed  information,  highlighting  the  province's  culinary  heritage  and  cultural  significance.  Finally,  the  website's  engaging  content  and  clear  contact  information  are  designed  to  attract  domestic  and  international  tourists  and  investors,  thereby  contributing  to  the  economic  development  of  Capiz.  Future  enhancements,  such  as  e-commerce  integration  and  multilingual  support,  could  further  strengthen  its  impact.
